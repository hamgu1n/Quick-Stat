# Unit 1 – Data Collection and Experimental Design

## Section 1.1 – Populations and Samples

In statistics we are often trying to answer questions about a population. You can think of a population as the target demographic we are interested in. Examples of population include: college students, employees at a company, or children ages 8–12. One of the most crucial aspects of statistics stems from our inability to examine a whole population of thousands of employees, millions of students, or over a billion children. Instead we sample populations, or take a small portion of the population to examine and draw conclusions about.

## Section 1.2 – Biased Sampling

Samples can be categorized as **biased** and **unbiased**. A biased sample is a sample where one portion of a population is favored or weighted more than others. Two common examples of biased samples include the **convenience sample** and the **voluntary response sample**. A convenience sample describes a sample of the easiest individuals to obtain, such as your friends, your coworkers, or the first 10 people to walk through a door. As you may notice these samples are by no means random and often contain large quantities of similar people.

A voluntary response sample only includes individuals who choose to include themselves in the study. This can include product surveys or reviews, RateMyProfessor comments, or surveys by companies to receive in store credit. Like a convenience sample, this can often lead to large numbers of the same types of people, for instance more outgoing people, individuals in need of class credits, or people motivated by quick cash.

**Example 1.2.1 – Convenience Sample**

Suppose a researcher wants to study students' favorite ice cream flavors. She surveys the first 20 students she meets outside the cafeteria.

Is this a biased or unbiased sample?

<Solution>

**Biased.** This is a convenience sample. Students who happen to be outside the cafeteria at that moment may not represent all students (for example, students who eat off campus or have class at that time are left out), so their flavor preferences may differ from the population's.

</Solution>
</div>

<div className="example">
**Example 1.2.2 – Voluntary Response Sample**

An online survey asks people to rate a new smartphone model. Only those who are motivated to respond will submit their opinions.

Explain why this may lead to a biased sample.

<Solution>

This is a voluntary response sample, so people **choose themselves** into the sample. People with strong opinions, especially very positive or very negative ones, are much more likely to respond than people who feel neutral. The responses will over-represent extreme opinions and won't reflect the typical buyer.

</Solution>

## Section 1.3 – Unbiased Sampling

In statistics, a good sample is measured by how representative it is of the general population we are examining. These are called **unbiased samples** and include: **stratified samples**, **systematic samples**, **cluster samples**, and **simple random samples**.

The most common sampling technique is the **simple random sample (SRS)**. An SRS consists of randomly selecting members of a population for your sample, as if picking names out of a hat.

Unlike a simple random sample, that does not require any framework, a **systematic sample** requires a predetermined method for sampling. Examples of a systematic sample include sampling every third person down a list of names, every fifth customer to enter a store, or every tenth house on a street. To reduce bias, the starting point of a sample is randomized.

Another example of an unbiased sample is a **stratified sample**. A stratified sample aims to reflect the demographic structure of a population. To take one, we first divide the population into groups that share a characteristic, called **strata** (singular: **stratum**), and then randomly select individuals from *every* stratum. For instance, a researcher may want to explore the sleeping habits of students at Roanoke College. Rather than simply selecting 50 students at random, the researcher may emphasize the importance of grade level in the sample. Using previously known demographic breakdowns, the researcher can randomly select a proportional number of students from each grade level.

Similar to stratified sampling, **cluster sampling** divides populations into groups called clusters. However, unlike stratified samples, cluster samples randomly select groups rather than individual observations. For instance, we can divide Roanoke College into clusters using dorm buildings. We can then select our sample by selecting whole dorm rooms, or rather all the students who live in a dorm building. We often use cluster sampling when our population is extremely large or spread out.

Each of these sampling methods has its place depending on the research question, the available resources, and the structure of the population. As we will later see, the choice of sampling method can have real consequences for the conclusions we draw from data.

**Example 1.3.1 – Simple Random Sample**

A teacher wants to randomly select 5 students out of a class of 30 for a quiz.

Describe how she could perform a simple random sample.

<Solution>

Number the students 1 to 30 (or put all 30 names on identical slips of paper). Then use a random number generator, or draw 5 slips from a well-mixed hat, to choose 5 students. Every student, and every possible group of 5 students, has the same chance of being chosen, which is what makes it a simple random sample.

</Solution>
</div>

<div className="example">
**Example 1.3.2 – Systematic Sample**

A librarian decides to check every 10th book on the shelf for damage.

Explain why this is a systematic sample.

<Solution>

The books are selected using a **fixed interval**: every 10th book in order on the shelf. That rule is what defines a systematic sample. (To make it random, the librarian should pick the starting book at random from the first 10.)

</Solution>
</div>

<div className="example">
**Example 1.3.3 – Stratified Sample**

A university wants to survey students about campus dining. There are 100 students in freshman year, 80 in sophomore year, 60 in junior year, and 40 in senior year.

If the researcher randomly selects 10% of students from each class, explain why this is a stratified sample.

<Solution>

The population is first divided into groups, called **strata** (freshmen, sophomores, juniors, and seniors), and then a random sample is taken *from each group*. That gives 10 freshmen, 8 sophomores, 6 juniors, and 4 seniors, 28 students in total. Every class year is guaranteed to be represented in proportion to its size.

</Solution>
</div>

<div className="example">
**Example 1.3.4 – Cluster Sample**

A city has 50 schools. To study student nutrition, the researcher randomly selects 5 schools and surveys all students in those schools.

Identify why this is a cluster sample.

<Solution>

The population is divided into natural groups, called **clusters** (the schools). The researcher randomly selects some clusters and then surveys **everyone** in the selected clusters. Randomly choosing whole groups, instead of choosing individuals from every group, is what makes this a cluster sample rather than a stratified sample.

</Solution>
</div>

<div className="example">
**Example 1.3.5 – Pick the Sampling Method**

For each scenario, identify the type of sampling method used (e.g., simple random, stratified, cluster, systematic, convenience):

1. A researcher surveys every 10th person who enters a library to ask about their reading habits.
2. A university divides students by major and randomly selects 10 students from each major to complete a survey.
3. A health study randomly selects 5 out of 20 classrooms in a school and surveys all students in the selected classrooms.
4. A pollster surveys friends and family about their favorite TV shows.
5. A company randomly selects 50 employees from its full employee roster for a survey on job satisfaction.
6. A researcher sends an online survey and collects responses only from participants who choose to respond.

<Solution>

1. **Systematic**: every 10th person.
2. **Stratified**: students are grouped by major, and a random sample is taken from every major.
3. **Cluster**: classrooms are randomly selected, and everyone in them is surveyed.
4. **Convenience**: friends and family are simply easy to reach.
5. **Simple random**: 50 employees chosen at random from the full roster.
6. **Voluntary response**: participants decide for themselves whether to respond.

</Solution>
</div>

<div className="example">
**Example 1.3.6 – Biased or Unbiased Sampling**

Determine if the following samples are biased or unbiased:

1. A survey conducted at a coffee shop asking about students' caffeine consumption.
2. A researcher randomly selects 20 students from a school roster to ask about their favorite subject.
3. Posting an online survey and letting anyone choose to respond about their study habits.

<Solution>

1. **Biased.** People at a coffee shop are likely to drink more caffeine than students in general.
2. **Unbiased.** A random selection from the full roster gives every student an equal chance of being chosen.
3. **Biased.** This is a voluntary response sample, so the people who choose to respond may differ from those who don't (for example, students who are especially proud of, or worried about, their study habits).

</Solution>

### Key Points – Populations and Samples
- A population is the full group we want to study; a sample is the portion we actually observe
- Biased samples over-represent some groups, convenience samples and voluntary response samples are common examples
- Unbiased sampling methods include simple random, systematic, stratified, and cluster sampling
- The goal of any sampling method is to produce a sample that accurately represents the population
- Choice of sampling method depends on the structure of the population and available resources
