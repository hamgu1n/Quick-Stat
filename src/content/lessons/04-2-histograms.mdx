## Section 2.6 – Histograms

When we move from categorical data to **numerical** (quantitative) data, bar charts are no longer the right tool. We need a way to visualize the *distribution* of a continuous variable, how observations are spread across a range of values. The **histogram** is the most fundamental tool for this job.

### What Is a Histogram?

A histogram divides the range of a numerical variable into equal-width intervals called **bins** (or classes), then draws a bar for each bin whose height represents how many observations fall within that range. Unlike bar charts, the bars in a histogram touch each other, this reflects the fact that the underlying data is continuous, with no gaps between possible values.

The choice of bin width matters enormously. Too few bins and you lose all the detail, the data looks like a featureless lump. Too many bins and the histogram becomes jagged and noisy, making patterns hard to see. In practice, statistical software chooses a reasonable default, but it is always worth experimenting. **Example 2.6.1 – Reading a Histogram**

A histogram of the ages of 60 participants at a community yoga class shows the following bin counts:

| Age Range | Count |
|---|---|
| 20–29 | 5 |
| 30–39 | 18 |

<Solution>

- The percentage between 40 and 49 is $\frac{22}{60} \approx 36.7\%$.
- The distribution is **roughly symmetric**. It peaks in the middle bin and tapers off on both sides, with slightly more participants just below the peak (18 in 30–39) than just above it (11 in 50–59).

**Example 2.6.2 – Effect of Bin Width**

A student creates two histograms of the same dataset, the number of hours 50 college students spent studying last week. The first uses 3 bins; the second uses 15 bins.

Describe the likely appearance of each histogram. Which would you prefer for an introductory report, and why?

<Solution>

- **3 bins:** too few. Almost all of the data are lumped into a few wide bars, hiding the shape of the distribution (any skew, gaps, or multiple peaks).
- **15 bins:** too many for only 50 observations. Many bins will have 0, 1, or 2 students, making the histogram look jagged and noisy.

For an introductory report, a **moderate number of bins (around 6 to 8)** usually works best. If forced to choose between these two, the 15-bin version at least shows detail, but neither is ideal.

</Solution>

### Describing the Shape of a Distribution

One of the main purposes of a histogram is to reveal the **shape** of a distribution. We describe shape using four key characteristics:

**Symmetric**: The left and right halves of the histogram are approximate mirror images. A perfect bell-shaped curve is symmetric. In symmetric distributions, the mean and median are close together.

**Skewed right** (positive skew): The histogram has a long tail stretching to the right. A few unusually large values pull the mean to the right of the median. Income distributions are a classic example, most people earn moderate incomes, but a few earn very high incomes.

**Skewed left** (negative skew): The histogram has a long tail stretching to the left. Exam scores in an easy class often look like this, most students score high, but a few score very low.

**Unimodal vs. bimodal**: A histogram with one clear peak is **unimodal**. A histogram with two peaks is **bimodal**, which often suggests the data comes from two distinct subgroups mixed together.

<div className="example">
**Example 2.6.3 – Identifying Shape**

For each scenario, predict whether the histogram would be symmetric, skewed right, or skewed left:

1. The prices of homes sold in a city last year, where most homes sell for 200,000 to 400,000 dollars but a handful sell for several million.
2. The scores on a very easy quiz where almost everyone got 9 or 10 out of 10.
3. The heights of adult men in the United States.
4. The time (in minutes) spent waiting in line at a grocery store, where most waits are short but occasional waits are very long.

<Solution>

1. **Skewed right**: a few multi-million-dollar homes stretch the right tail.
2. **Skewed left**: scores pile up near the maximum of 10, with a tail of lower scores to the left.
3. **Symmetric**: heights are roughly bell-shaped.
4. **Skewed right**: most waits are short, with occasional very long waits stretching the right tail.

</Solution>
</div>

<div className="example">
**Example 2.6.4 – Mean vs. Median from a Histogram**

A histogram of daily tips earned by servers at a restaurant is strongly skewed to the right. Without calculating, predict whether the mean or the median will be larger. Explain why.

<Solution>

The **mean will be larger** than the median. In a right-skewed distribution, a few very large tips pull the mean toward the long right tail, while the median only depends on the middle value and is resistant to those extremes.

</Solution>
</div>

<div className="example">
**Example 2.6.5 – Constructing a Histogram**

The following are the number of text messages sent by 20 students in a single day:

$$
5, 12, 8, 22, 3, 17, 9, 14, 30, 6, 11, 18, 25, 7, 10, 15, 20, 4, 13, 16
$$

Group the data into bins of width 10 (0–9, 10–19, 20–29, 30–39). How many observations fall in each bin? Describe the shape of the resulting histogram.

<Solution>

| Bin | Values | Count |
|---|---|---|
| 0–9 | 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 | 7 |
| 10–19 | 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 | 9 |
| 20–29 | 20, 22, 25 | 3 |
| 30–39 | 30 | 1 |

The histogram peaks in the 10–19 bin, and then the counts drop off with a tail toward higher values, so the distribution is **skewed right**. Most students send fewer than 20 texts, with a few sending many more.

</Solution>

### Key Points – Histograms
- Histograms visualize the distribution of **numerical** data by grouping values into bins
- Bars touch because the data is continuous; height represents frequency or relative frequency within each bin
- Bin width affects how much detail is visible, too few or too many bins obscures the true shape
- Distributions are described as symmetric, skewed right (long right tail), or skewed left (long left tail)
- In a right-skewed distribution the mean is greater than the median; in a left-skewed distribution the mean is less than the median
- Bimodal histograms (two peaks) may indicate that two distinct groups are present in the data
