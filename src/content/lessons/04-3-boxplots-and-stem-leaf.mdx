## Section 4.3 – Boxplots and Stem-and-Leaf Plots

Histograms are excellent for showing the overall shape of a distribution, but they hide the individual data values. Two other graphical tools, the **boxplot** and the **stem-and-leaf plot**, offer different trade-offs between showing shape and preserving detail.

### Boxplots

A **boxplot** (sometimes called a box-and-whisker plot) is a visual display of the five-number summary we introduced in Section 3.4. The components of a boxplot are:

- A **box** that spans from $Q_1$ to $Q_3$, representing the middle 50% of the data (the IQR)
- A **line inside the box** at the median ($Q_2$)
- **Whiskers** that extend from the box out to the smallest and largest non-outlier values
- **Individual points** plotted beyond the whiskers for any flagged outliers (using the IQR rule from Section 3.3)

Boxplots are particularly powerful for **comparing two or more groups side by side**. When placed next to each other on the same axis, boxplots make it easy to see whether the medians differ, whether one group is more spread out, and whether one group has more outliers. **Example 4.3.1 – Reading a Boxplot**

A boxplot for the number of hours students studied last week shows:

- Minimum: 0, $Q_1$: 3, Median: 6, $Q_3$: 9, Maximum: 14, with one outlier at 25

Interpret this boxplot. What does the position of the median inside the box suggest about the shape of the distribution?

<Solution>

- The middle 50% of students studied between 3 and 9 hours (IQR = 6 hours), and the median is 6 hours.
- Excluding the outlier, study time ranged from 0 to 14 hours.
- One student studied 25 hours, far beyond the rest, and is plotted as a separate outlier point.

The median sits **exactly in the middle of the box**, which suggests the middle half of the data is roughly symmetric. The high outlier, and the longer upper whisker (5 hours, vs. 3 hours for the lower whisker), point to some right skew in the tail.

</Solution>

**Example 4.3.2 – Constructing a Boxplot**

The following are the prices (in dollars) of 11 textbooks at a college bookstore:

$$
28, 35, 42, 55, 60, 65, 70, 78, 85, 92, 180
$$

Find the five-number summary. Use the IQR rule to check whether 180 is a flagged outlier. Then describe how the boxplot would look.

<Solution>

With $n = 11$:

- Min = 28
- $Q_1$ = median of $28, 35, 42, 55, 60$ = 42
- Median = 65
- $Q_3$ = median of $70, 78, 85, 92, 180$ = 85
- Max = 180

**Outlier check:** IQR $= 85 - 42 = 43$, so the upper fence is $85 + 1.5(43) = 149.5$ (the lower fence is $42 - 64.5 = -22.5$). Since $180 > 149.5$, **180 is a flagged outlier**.

**The boxplot:** the box runs from 42 to 85 with a line at 65. The lower whisker extends to 28, and the upper whisker stops at 92 (the largest value that isn't an outlier). The value 180 is plotted as a separate point far to the right.

</Solution>
</div>

**Example 4.3.3 – Comparing Groups with Boxplots**

Two coffee shops record their daily customer counts over 30 days. Shop A has a median of 120 customers, $Q_1 = 100$, and $Q_3 = 140$. Shop B has a median of 115 customers, $Q_1 = 80$, and $Q_3 = 160$.

Both shops have roughly the same median. What does the IQR tell us about which shop is more consistent day to day?

<Solution>

**Shop A is more consistent.** Its IQR is $140 - 100 = 40$ customers, while Shop B's IQR is $160 - 80 = 80$ customers. The middle half of Shop B's days varies twice as much as Shop A's, even though their typical (median) day is about the same. Shop A can plan staffing more reliably.

</Solution>

### Stem-and-Leaf Plots

A **stem-and-leaf plot** is a simple way to display the distribution of a small dataset while preserving every individual value. Each observation is split into a **stem** (the leading digit or digits) and a **leaf** (the final digit). The stems are listed in a column on the left, and the corresponding leaves are written to the right.

For example, the value 74 would have a stem of 7 and a leaf of 4. The value 112 could have a stem of 11 and a leaf of 2.

Stem-and-leaf plots are most useful for small datasets (roughly 10 to 50 observations) where you want to see the full distribution *and* retain the original values. They work best for two-digit and three-digit numbers. One clever extension is a **back-to-back stem-and-leaf plot**, which places two groups on either side of a shared set of stems, making comparison as easy as reading a boxplot but with every data value visible.

<div className="example">
**Example 4.3.4 – Constructing a Stem-and-Leaf Plot**

The following are the quiz scores of 15 students:

$$
62, 75, 81, 73, 68, 90, 77, 84, 71, 65, 88, 92, 79, 83, 66
$$

Organize these scores into a stem-and-leaf plot using stems 6, 7, 8, and 9. What does the plot reveal about the shape of the distribution?

<Solution>

Sorted scores: $62, 65, 66, 68, 71, 73, 75, 77, 79, 81, 83, 84, 88, 90, 92$

| Stem | Leaves |
|---|---|
| 6 | 2 5 6 8 |
| 7 | 1 3 5 7 9 |
| 8 | 1 3 4 8 |
| 9 | 0 2 |

Key: 6 | 2 = 62

The distribution is **roughly symmetric** (perhaps slightly skewed right). It is mound-shaped with a peak in the 70s, where 5 of the 15 scores fall, and the median is 77.

</Solution>
</div>

**Example 4.3.5 – Back-to-Back Stem-and-Leaf**

The number of pushups completed by students in two gym classes is recorded below.

Class 1: 18, 22, 25, 27, 30, 31, 33, 35, 38, 40
Class 2: 20, 23, 24, 26, 28, 29, 32, 34, 36, 41

Create a back-to-back stem-and-leaf plot. Which class appears to have a higher typical number of pushups?

<Solution>

Class 1's leaves go on the left (read right to left) and Class 2's leaves go on the right:

| Class 1 | Stem | Class 2 |
|---:|:---:|:---|
| 8 | 1 | |
| 7 5 2 | 2 | 0 3 4 6 8 9 |
| 8 5 3 1 0 | 3 | 2 4 6 |
| 0 | 4 | 1 |

Key: 8 | 1 | means 18 for Class 1, and | 2 | 0 means 20 for Class 2.

**Class 1** appears to have a slightly higher typical number of pushups. Most of its values sit in the 30s, while most of Class 2's are in the 20s. The medians confirm this: 30.5 for Class 1 vs. 28.5 for Class 2.

### Key Points – Boxplots and Stem-and-Leaf Plots
- A boxplot displays the five-number summary: minimum, $Q_1$, median, $Q_3$, and maximum
- The box spans the IQR; the line inside marks the median; whiskers extend to the most extreme non-outlier values
- Outliers flagged by the IQR rule are shown as individual points beyond the whiskers
- Boxplots excel at comparing two or more groups side by side
- A stem-and-leaf plot preserves individual values while showing the distribution, useful for small datasets
- Back-to-back stem-and-leaf plots compare two groups using a shared set of stems
