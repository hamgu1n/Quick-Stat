## Section 4.4 – Scatterplots

Every graph we have looked at so far has displayed a single variable at a time, its distribution, its center, its spread. But many of the most interesting questions in statistics involve the *relationship* between two variables. Does studying more lead to higher grades? Do taller people tend to weigh more? Does higher caffeine consumption correlate with better test performance? To explore these questions visually, we use a **scatterplot**.

### What Is a Scatterplot?

A **scatterplot** displays pairs of numerical observations as points on a coordinate plane. One variable is placed on the horizontal axis (the **explanatory variable**, also called the independent variable) and the other on the vertical axis (the **response variable**, also called the dependent variable). Each dot on the plot represents one observation in the dataset.

When reading a scatterplot, we focus on three features of the relationship:

1. **Direction**: Does the relationship go upward (as $x$ increases, $y$ tends to increase, a **positive association**) or downward (as $x$ increases, $y$ tends to decrease, a **negative association**)?
2. **Form**: Is the pattern roughly **linear** (the points follow a straight-line trend) or **curved**?
3. **Strength**: How closely do the points follow the trend? Are they tightly packed around an imaginary line, or are they loosely scattered? We will quantify strength more precisely in Chapter 10 using correlation coefficients.

**Example 4.4.1 – Reading a Scatterplot**

A scatterplot shows the number of hours employees work per week on the horizontal axis and their self-reported stress level (on a scale of 1 to 10) on the vertical axis. The points form an upward-sloping cloud.

Describe the direction, form, and approximate strength of the relationship. What would you expect to happen to stress levels as work hours increase?

<Solution>

- **Direction:** positive, since stress tends to rise as work hours increase.
- **Form:** roughly linear, based on the upward-sloping cloud.
- **Strength:** a "cloud" suggests a **moderate** relationship. The points follow the trend but with noticeable scatter.

We would expect stress levels to **increase, on average**, as work hours increase. Individual employees will vary, so not everyone who works more will report more stress.

For each pair of variables, predict whether the scatterplot would show a positive association, a negative association, or no clear association:

1. Average daily temperature and hot coffee sales at a café.

### Outliers in Scatterplots

Just as we look for outliers in single-variable distributions, we also watch for them in scatterplots. An outlier in a scatterplot is a point that falls far from the overall trend, either because its $x$ value, its $y$ value, or both are unusual. Some outliers are also called **influential points** because, as we will see in Chapter 11, they can dramatically shift the line we fit through the data.

It's important not to remove outliers automatically. Sometimes they represent data entry errors; other times they are genuine and scientifically important observations that deserve special attention.

<div className="example">
**Example 4.4.3 – Identifying an Outlier in a Scatterplot**

A researcher plots the number of hours students spent on a take-home project (x-axis) against the score they received (y-axis). Most points form an upward-sloping cloud between 2 and 10 hours. One student spent 1 hour and received a 95. Another spent 12 hours and received a 40.

Are either of these points outliers? Could they be influential? How might they affect a line drawn through the data?

<Solution>

**Both points are outliers**, since each falls far from the upward trend of the other points.

- The student who spent **1 hour and scored 95** has an unusually low $x$-value *and* an unusually high $y$-value for that $x$.
- The student who spent **12 hours and scored 40** is beyond the range of the other $x$-values and far below the trend.

Both could be **influential**, because they sit at the edges of the $x$-range where they have the most pull on a line. Each would flatten the line: the 1-hour point raises its left end, and the 12-hour point drags its right end down. Together they could make the relationship look much weaker than it really is for most students.

</Solution>
</div>

**Example 4.4.4 – Describing a Scatterplot**

The following data show the weekly advertising spend (in hundreds of dollars) and weekly sales (in thousands of dollars) for a small bakery:

| Advertising | Sales |
|---|---|
| 1 | 3.2 |
| 2 | 5.1 |
| 3 | 6.8 |
| 5 | 9.4 |
| 7 | 12.1 |
| 8 | 11.8 |
| 10 | 15.3 |

Describe the direction, form, and strength of the relationship. What would you predict for sales if the bakery spent 600 dollars on advertising?

<Solution>

- **Direction:** positive, since sales increase as advertising increases.
- **Form:** linear, as the points follow a nearly straight line.
- **Strength:** very strong. The points lie close to a line (one small dip at 8, where sales are 11.8, a little below the 12.1 at 7).

At an advertising spend of 600 dollars ($x = 6$ hundred), sales should fall between the values at $x = 5$ (9.4) and $x = 7$ (12.1), so we would predict about **10 to 11 thousand dollars** in weekly sales. (A least-squares line, from Chapter 11, gives about 10.2 thousand dollars.)

</Solution>
</div>

<div className="example">
**Example 4.4.5 – Causation vs. Association**

A scatterplot of ice cream sales (x-axis) and drowning rates (y-axis) across months of the year shows a strong positive association, months with high ice cream sales also tend to have more drownings.

Does this mean eating ice cream causes drowning? What is a more reasonable explanation? What concept from Chapter 2 is at work here?

<Solution>

**No.** Association does not prove causation. A more reasonable explanation is a **lurking (confounding) variable**: **hot weather**. In summer, people buy more ice cream *and* swim more often, which leads to more drownings. The temperature drives both variables, so they rise and fall together even though neither causes the other.

This is the distinction from Chapter 2 between **observational studies and experiments**. These data are observational, so we can't draw cause-and-effect conclusions from them.

</Solution>
</div>

### Key Points – Scatterplots
- Scatterplots display the relationship between two numerical variables; each point represents one observation
- The explanatory variable goes on the x-axis; the response variable goes on the y-axis
- Describe a relationship by its direction (positive or negative), form (linear or curved), and strength (tight or loose)
- A positive association means both variables tend to move in the same direction; a negative association means they move in opposite directions
- Outliers in a scatterplot fall far from the overall trend and may be influential points
- Association does not imply causation, a lurking variable may drive both observed variables
