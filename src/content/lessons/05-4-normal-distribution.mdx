## Section 3.4 – The Normal Distribution

So far, our probability rules have dealt with outcomes we can list and count, like rolling a die or drawing a card. But many of the variables we care about, like heights, test scores, or the time it takes a web page to load, are measured on a continuous scale. For these, we need a *probability distribution*, a model that tells us how likely different ranges of values are. The most important of these models, and the one that shows up again and again in statistics, is the **normal distribution**.

### The Normal Curve

A **normal distribution** is a symmetric, bell-shaped distribution that is completely described by two parameters: its mean $\mu$ and its standard deviation $\sigma$. We write $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)$, read "X follows a normal distribution with mean mu and standard deviation sigma," to say that the variable $X$ follows a normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$.

Every normal curve shares the same basic features:

- It is symmetric around the mean, so the mean, median, and mode are all equal
- It is highest at the mean and tapers off equally in both directions
- The total area under the curve is 1, representing 100% of the probability
- The tails extend forever in both directions but get very close to zero within a few standard deviations of the mean

For a continuous variable, probability is represented by **area under the curve**. The probability that $X$ falls between two values $a$ and $b$ is the area under the normal curve between $a$ and $b$. One consequence of this is that the probability of any single exact value is 0, so $P(X \leq a)$ and $P(X < a)$ are the same for a normal distribution.

You already saw in Section 2.2 how changing $\mu$ shifts the curve and changing $\sigma$ widens or narrows it. No matter where it is centered or how spread out it is, the *shape* is always the same bell curve.

### The Empirical Rule

Because every normal curve has the same shape, the same proportion of the data always falls within a given number of standard deviations of the mean. This gives us a quick rule of thumb called the **Empirical Rule**, or the **68-95-99.7 Rule**:

- About **68%** of values fall within 1 standard deviation of the mean, between $\mu - \sigma$ and $\mu + \sigma$
- About **95%** of values fall within 2 standard deviations of the mean, between $\mu - 2\sigma$ and $\mu + 2\sigma$
- About **99.7%** of values fall within 3 standard deviations of the mean, between $\mu - 3\sigma$ and $\mu + 3\sigma$

This rule is useful for quick estimates and for spotting unusual values. A value more than 2 standard deviations from the mean is fairly unusual, and a value more than 3 standard deviations away is very rare.

<div className="example">
**Example 3.4.1 – Using the Empirical Rule**

The time users spend on a social media app per session is approximately normally distributed with a mean of 22 minutes and a standard deviation of 6 minutes.

1. Between what two values do about 68% of session times fall?
2. Between what two values do about 95% of session times fall?
3. About what percentage of sessions last longer than 34 minutes?

<Solution>

With $\mu = 22$ and $\sigma = 6$:

1. **About 68%** fall within 1 SD: between $22 - 6 = 16$ and $22 + 6 = 28$ minutes.
2. **About 95%** fall within 2 SDs: between $22 - 12 = 10$ and $22 + 12 = 34$ minutes.
3. 34 minutes is exactly 2 SDs above the mean. About 95% of sessions are within 2 SDs, so 5% are outside, split evenly between the two tails. **About 2.5%** of sessions last longer than 34 minutes.

</Solution>

### The Standard Normal Distribution

Since there are infinitely many normal distributions, one for every combination of $\mu$ and $\sigma$, it would be impossible to build a separate table for each one. Instead, we convert any normal variable to a single reference distribution called the **standard normal distribution**, which has a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1, written $Z \sim N(0, 1)$.

We make this conversion with the z-score formula from Section 2.3, written here with the population mean and standard deviation:

$$
z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}
$$

The z-score tells us how many standard deviations $x$ is from the mean. Once a value has been converted to a z-score, we can find probabilities using a **z-table** (a table of areas under the standard normal curve) or using software. Both give the area to the *left* of a value.

### Finding Probabilities

To find a probability for a normal variable, follow three steps:

1. Draw a sketch of the normal curve and shade the area you want
2. Convert the value(s) to z-scores
3. Use a z-table or software to find the shaded area

Since z-tables and software both give the area to the left, the area to the right of a value is found with the complement rule from Section 3.1:

$$
P(Z > z) = 1 - P(Z < z)
$$

and the area between two values is the difference of two left areas:

$$
P(a < Z < b) = P(Z < b) - P(Z < a)
$$

<div className="example">
**Example 3.4.2 – Finding a Probability**

SAT math scores are approximately normally distributed with a mean of 520 and a standard deviation of 115.

1. What proportion of students score below 400?
2. What proportion of students score above 700?
3. What proportion of students score between 450 and 600?

<Solution>

With $\mu = 520$ and $\sigma = 115$:

1. $z = \dfrac{400 - 520}{115} \approx -1.04$, so $P(X < 400) = P(Z < -1.04) \approx 0.148$. About **14.8%** of students score below 400.
2. $z = \dfrac{700 - 520}{115} \approx 1.57$, so $P(X > 700) = 1 - P(Z < 1.57) \approx 0.059$. About **5.9%** of students score above 700.
3. $z_1 = \dfrac{450 - 520}{115} \approx -0.61$ and $z_2 = \dfrac{600 - 520}{115} \approx 0.70$, so $P(450 < X < 600) = P(Z < 0.70) - P(Z < -0.61) \approx 0.485$. About **48.5%** of students score between 450 and 600.

*Answers from a z-table may differ slightly in the third decimal place because the z-scores are rounded.*

</Solution>
</div>

<div className="example">
**Example 3.4.3 – Comparing Using the Normal Model**

An online retailer finds that delivery times for standard shipping are approximately normal with a mean of 4.2 days and a standard deviation of 0.9 days.

1. What is the probability that a package arrives in under 3 days?
2. The retailer advertises "delivery within 6 days." What percentage of packages fail to meet this promise?

<Solution>

With $\mu = 4.2$ and $\sigma = 0.9$:

1. $z = \dfrac{3 - 4.2}{0.9} \approx -1.33$, so $P(X < 3) = P(Z < -1.33) \approx 0.091$. About **9.1%** of packages arrive in under 3 days.
2. $z = \dfrac{6 - 4.2}{0.9} = 2$, so $P(X > 6) = 1 - P(Z < 2) \approx 0.023$. About **2.3%** of packages fail to meet the 6-day promise.

</Solution>

### Finding Percentiles

Sometimes we want to work in the opposite direction. Instead of starting with a value and finding a probability, we start with a probability and find the value. For example, what score does a student need to be in the top 10%? This is a question about a **percentile**.

To find a percentile:

1. Find the z-score that has the desired area to its left (using a z-table in reverse, or software)
2. Convert the z-score back to the original units by solving the z-score formula for $x$:

$$
x = \mu + z\sigma
$$

<div className="example">
**Example 3.4.4 – Finding a Percentile**

Using the SAT math scores from Example 3.4.2 (mean 520, standard deviation 115):

1. What score marks the 90th percentile?
2. A scholarship program accepts only students in the top 2.5%. What is the minimum score needed?

<Solution>

1. The 90th percentile has area 0.90 to its left, which corresponds to $z \approx 1.28$:

$$
x = \mu + z\sigma = 520 + 1.28(115) \approx 667
$$

2. The top 2.5% starts at the 97.5th percentile, $z \approx 1.96$:

$$
x = 520 + 1.96(115) \approx 745
$$

A student needs a score of about **745** to qualify for the scholarship.

</Solution>
</div>

### Is the Normal Model Appropriate?

The normal distribution is a *model*, and like any model, it only works when the data actually look approximately normal. Before using normal calculations, we should check:

- **A histogram** of the data should look roughly symmetric and bell-shaped, without strong skew or multiple peaks
- **A normal probability plot** (also called a Q-Q plot) plots the data against the values we would expect from a normal distribution. If the data are approximately normal, the points fall close to a straight line. Curves or bends in the plot indicate skewness, and points far off the line at the ends indicate unusually heavy tails or outliers

### Key Points – The Normal Distribution
- A normal distribution $N(\mu, \sigma)$ is symmetric and bell-shaped, and is completely determined by its mean and standard deviation
- For continuous variables, probability is the area under the curve; the total area is 1
- The Empirical Rule (68-95-99.7 Rule): about 68%, 95%, and 99.7% of values fall within 1, 2, and 3 standard deviations of the mean
- Any normal variable can be standardized with $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$, giving the standard normal distribution $N(0, 1)$
- Z-tables and software give the area to the left; use the complement rule for right-tail areas
- To find a percentile, find the z-score first and then convert back with $x = \mu + z\sigma$
- Always check that a normal model is reasonable using a histogram or normal probability plot before using normal calculations
