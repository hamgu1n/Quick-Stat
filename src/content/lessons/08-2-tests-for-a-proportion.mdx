## Section 8.2 – Hypothesis Tests for a Proportion

Many of the most common questions in statistics are about proportions rather than means. What fraction of users click on a new website button? What percentage of voters support a ballot measure? Has the share of adults who get their news from social media changed? In Section 7.2 we built confidence intervals for a proportion. In this section we use the hypothesis testing framework from Section 8.1 to test claims about a proportion.

### Setting Up the Test

A hypothesis test for a proportion compares the sample proportion $\hat{p}$ to a claimed value of the population proportion, which we call $p_0$. The hypotheses take one of three forms:

- $H_0$: $p = p_0$ vs. $H_a$: $p \neq p_0$ (two-tailed)
- $H_0$: $p = p_0$ vs. $H_a$: $p > p_0$ (right-tailed)
- $H_0$: $p = p_0$ vs. $H_a$: $p < p_0$ (left-tailed)

The null hypothesis always contains the equals sign, and the hypotheses are always about the population proportion $p$, never the sample proportion $\hat{p}$. We already know what $\hat{p}$ is, since we calculated it from our data.

<div className="example">
**Example 8.2.1 – Writing Hypotheses for a Proportion**

For each scenario, define the parameter $p$ in words and write $H_0$ and $H_a$:

1. A company claims that 80% of its customers are satisfied. A consumer group suspects the true percentage is lower.
2. Historically, 3% of visitors to a website make a purchase. After a redesign, the company wants to know whether the purchase rate has changed.
3. A news article claims that more than half of teenagers use social media for more than 3 hours a day.

<Solution>

1. $p$ = the true proportion of the company's customers who are satisfied. $H_0: p = 0.80$ vs. $H_a: p < 0.80$
2. $p$ = the true proportion of visitors who make a purchase after the redesign. $H_0: p = 0.03$ vs. $H_a: p \neq 0.03$
3. $p$ = the true proportion of teenagers who use social media for more than 3 hours a day. $H_0: p = 0.50$ vs. $H_a: p > 0.50$

</Solution>
</div>

### Conditions for the Test

Like the confidence interval for a proportion, the test relies on the sampling distribution of $\hat{p}$ being approximately normal. Before carrying out the test, we check two conditions:

1. **Independence**: the observations are independent. This is reasonable when the data come from a random sample (or a randomized experiment), and the sample is less than 10% of the population.
2. **Success-failure condition**: we expect at least 10 successes and 10 failures *if the null hypothesis is true*:

$$
np_0 \geq 10 \qquad \text{and} \qquad n(1 - p_0) \geq 10
$$

Notice that we use $p_0$ here, not $\hat{p}$. A hypothesis test is carried out assuming $H_0$ is true, so we use the value that $H_0$ claims. This is a key difference from the confidence interval, where we have no claimed value and must use $\hat{p}$ instead.

### The One-Proportion z-Test

When the conditions are met, the test statistic is:

$$
z = \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{p_0(1 - p_0)}{n}}}
$$

The denominator is the **standard error** of $\hat{p}$ calculated under the assumption that $H_0$ is true. Just like the z-test for a mean, this statistic tells us how many standard errors the sample proportion falls from the claimed proportion. Under $H_0$, it follows the standard normal distribution, so we find the p-value using the methods from Section 5.4:

- **Right-tailed**: p-value $= P(Z > z)$
- **Left-tailed**: p-value $= P(Z < z)$
- **Two-tailed**: p-value $= 2 \times P(Z > |z|)$

Then we compare the p-value to $\alpha$ and state a conclusion in context, exactly as in Section 8.1.

<div className="example">
**Example 8.2.2 – One-Proportion z-Test**

A social media platform claims that 40% of its users log in every day. A researcher takes a random sample of 500 users and finds that 182 of them logged in every day.

At $\alpha = 0.05$, is there evidence that the true proportion of daily users is different from 40%?

1. State $H_0$ and $H_a$, defining $p$ in words.
2. Check the conditions.
3. Calculate $\hat{p}$ and the z-test statistic.
4. Find the p-value.
5. State your conclusion in context.

<Solution>

1. $p$ = the true proportion of all users who log in every day. $H_0: p = 0.40$ vs. $H_a: p \neq 0.40$
2. **Independence:** random sample, and 500 is less than 10% of all users. **Success-failure (using $p_0$):** $500(0.40) = 200 \geq 10$ and $500(0.60) = 300 \geq 10$. Both conditions are met.
3. $\hat{p} = \dfrac{182}{500} = 0.364$, and

$$
z = \frac{0.364 - 0.40}{\sqrt{\dfrac{0.40(0.60)}{500}}} = \frac{-0.036}{0.0219} \approx -1.64
$$

4. p-value $= 2 \times P(Z < -1.64) \approx 0.100$
5. Since $0.100 > 0.05$, we **fail to reject $H_0$**. The sample proportion (36.4%) is below 40%, but not by enough to rule out chance. We don't have convincing evidence that the platform's 40% claim is wrong.

</Solution>
</div>

<div className="example">
**Example 8.2.3 – A One-Tailed Test**

An online retailer's checkout page historically converts 12% of visitors into buyers. After adding a "free shipping" banner, the retailer tracks 850 randomly selected visitors and finds that 124 make a purchase.

At $\alpha = 0.05$, is there evidence that the banner *increased* the conversion rate?

1. State $H_0$ and $H_a$.
2. Check the conditions.
3. Calculate the test statistic and p-value.
4. State your conclusion in context. Can the retailer conclude that the banner *caused* the change? What would they need to know about how the data were collected?

<Solution>

1. $H_0: p = 0.12$ vs. $H_a: p > 0.12$, where $p$ is the conversion rate with the banner.
2. **Independence:** randomly selected visitors. **Success-failure:** $850(0.12) = 102 \geq 10$ and $850(0.88) = 748 \geq 10$.
3. $\hat{p} = \dfrac{124}{850} \approx 0.1459$, and

$$
z = \frac{0.1459 - 0.12}{\sqrt{\dfrac{0.12(0.88)}{850}}} = \frac{0.0259}{0.0111} \approx 2.32
$$

   p-value $= P(Z > 2.32) \approx 0.010$.

4. Since $0.010 \leq 0.05$, we **reject $H_0$**. There is evidence that the conversion rate is now higher than 12%.

   However, **we can't conclude that the banner *caused* the increase** from these data alone. Comparing to a historical rate means something else could also have changed (season, a sale, different traffic sources). To show causation, the retailer would need a **randomized experiment** that shows some visitors the banner and others not at the same time, which is an A/B test (Section 8.3).

</Solution>
</div>

### Connecting Tests and Confidence Intervals

Confidence intervals and two-tailed tests are closely linked. A 95% confidence interval contains the values of $p$ that are plausible based on our data. If $p_0$ falls *outside* the 95% interval, a two-tailed test at $\alpha = 0.05$ will reject $H_0$. If $p_0$ falls *inside* the interval, the test will fail to reject $H_0$.

The match is not always exact for proportions, since the interval uses $\hat{p}$ in its standard error while the test uses $p_0$. But the two methods almost always lead to the same conclusion, and the confidence interval has an advantage: it tells us not only *whether* $p$ differs from $p_0$, but by *how much*.

<div className="example">
**Example 8.2.4 – Interval or Test?**

A poll of 1,200 randomly selected adults finds that 54% support a new data privacy law. A 95% confidence interval for the true proportion is (0.512, 0.568).

1. Based on the interval, would a two-tailed test at $\alpha = 0.05$ reject $H_0$: $p = 0.50$? Explain.
2. Would it reject $H_0$: $p = 0.55$?
3. What does the interval tell you that the test alone does not?

<Solution>

1. **Yes.** The value 0.50 is **outside** the interval (0.512, 0.568), so 50% is not a plausible value for $p$, and a two-tailed test at $\alpha = 0.05$ would reject $H_0: p = 0.50$.
2. **No.** The value 0.55 is **inside** the interval, so it is a plausible value, and the test would fail to reject $H_0: p = 0.55$.
3. The interval shows the **range of plausible values** and the size of the effect. Support is likely between about 51% and 57%, which is a narrow majority, not just "different from 50%."

</Solution>
</div>

### Choosing a Sample Size

Before collecting data, researchers often want to know how large a sample they need to estimate a proportion with a certain margin of error. The margin of error for a proportion is

$$
ME = z^* \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}
$$

Solving this for $n$ gives

$$
n = \left(\frac{z^*}{ME}\right)^2 p(1 - p)
$$

But there is a catch: we don't know $p$ before collecting the data. If we have a reasonable estimate from a previous study, we use it. If not, we use $p = 0.5$, because $p(1-p)$ is largest when $p = 0.5$. This gives the most conservative (largest) sample size, guaranteeing our margin of error is no larger than the target. Always round $n$ *up* to the next whole number.

<div className="example">
**Example 8.2.5 – Sample Size for a Proportion**

A campus newspaper wants to estimate the proportion of students who use a smartphone app to order food, with a margin of error of 4 percentage points at the 95% confidence level.

1. How many students should they survey if they have no prior estimate of $p$?
2. A survey last year found that about 30% of students used such an app. How many students should they survey using this estimate?
3. Why is the answer to part 2 smaller than the answer to part 1?

<Solution>

With $z^* = 1.96$ and $ME = 0.04$:

1. No prior estimate, so use $p = 0.5$:

$$
n = \left(\frac{1.96}{0.04}\right)^2 (0.5)(0.5) = 2401 \times 0.25 = 600.25 \quad\Rightarrow\quad n = 601 \text{ students}
$$

2. With $p = 0.30$:

$$
n = 2401 \times (0.30)(0.70) = 504.21 \quad\Rightarrow\quad n = 505 \text{ students}
$$

3. $p(1-p)$ is largest at $p = 0.5$ (where it equals 0.25). At $p = 0.30$, it is only 0.21, so less variability is expected and fewer students are needed. Using $p = 0.5$ is the "safe" choice when we have no information.

</Solution>
</div>

### Statistical vs. Practical Significance

With a very large sample, even a tiny difference between $\hat{p}$ and $p_0$ can produce a small p-value. For example, a website with millions of visitors might find that a new button color increases the click rate from 5.00% to 5.03%, with a p-value well below 0.05. The result is **statistically significant**, meaning it is unlikely to be due to chance alone, but it may not be **practically significant**, meaning large enough to matter in the real world. A good analysis reports both the p-value and the size of the effect, which is another reason confidence intervals are so useful.

<div className="example">
**Example 8.2.6 – Is It Meaningful?**

A large online study of 2,000,000 users finds that a redesigned sign-up page raises the sign-up rate from 8.0% to 8.1%, with a p-value of 0.0001.

1. Is this result statistically significant at $\alpha = 0.05$?
2. Is it practically significant? What other information would help a company decide whether the redesign is worth it?

<Solution>

1. **Yes.** $0.0001 < 0.05$, so the result is statistically significant. With 2 million users, even a tiny difference is very unlikely to be due to chance.
2. **Probably not, by itself.** A 0.1 percentage point increase means about 1 extra sign-up per 1,000 visitors. Whether that matters depends on information such as:
   - the cost of building and maintaining the redesign
   - the value of each new sign-up (for example, revenue per user)
   - how many visitors the page gets in total
   - whether the redesign affects anything else (user satisfaction, later cancellations)

</Solution>
</div>

### Key Points – Hypothesis Tests for a Proportion
- Hypotheses are always about the population proportion $p$; the null hypothesis is $H_0$: $p = p_0$
- Check independence and the success-failure condition using $p_0$: $np_0 \geq 10$ and $n(1-p_0) \geq 10$
- Test statistic: $z = (\hat{p} - p_0) / \sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}$, which uses $p_0$ in the standard error
- Find the p-value from the standard normal distribution, then compare to $\alpha$ and state the conclusion in context
- A two-tailed test at level $\alpha$ and a $(1 - \alpha)$ confidence interval almost always agree, but the interval also shows the size of the effect
- To plan a study, use $n = (z^*/ME)^2 \, p(1-p)$ with $p = 0.5$ if no prior estimate is available, and round up
- Statistical significance does not guarantee practical significance, especially with very large samples
