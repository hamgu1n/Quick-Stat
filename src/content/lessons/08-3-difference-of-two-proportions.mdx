## Section 4.5 – Difference of Two Proportions

In the previous section we compared a single proportion to a claimed value. But many of the most interesting questions involve *comparing two groups*. Does a new website layout get a higher click rate than the old one? Are younger adults more likely than older adults to get their news from social media? Did a vaccine group get sick less often than a placebo group? Each of these compares two population proportions, $p_1$ and $p_2$ (pronounced "p-one" and "p-two"), and the quantity we care about is their difference, $p_1 - p_2$.

### A/B Testing

Comparing two proportions is the statistics behind **A/B testing**, one of the most common tools used by tech companies and online retailers. In an A/B test, visitors to a website are randomly assigned to see either version A or version B of a page, and the company compares the proportion of visitors in each group who take some action, like clicking a button or making a purchase.

Because visitors are *randomly assigned*, an A/B test is a randomized experiment. As we saw in Section 1.5, this means that if version B performs significantly better, we can conclude that the change *caused* the improvement, not just that the two are associated.

### The Sampling Distribution of $\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2$

Our point estimate for $p_1 - p_2$ is the difference in sample proportions, $\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2$ (read "p-hat one minus p-hat two"). Like a single sample proportion, this difference varies from sample to sample. When the conditions below are met, its sampling distribution is approximately normal with standard error

$$
SE = \sqrt{\frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1} + \frac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2}}
$$

Notice that the variances of the two groups *add*, even though we are subtracting the proportions. Each sample adds its own uncertainty, so the difference is more uncertain than either proportion alone.

The conditions are:

1. **Independence within groups**: the observations in each group are independent (for example, from random samples or random assignment)
2. **Independence between groups**: the two groups are independent of each other, meaning the data are not paired
3. **Success-failure condition**: there are at least 10 successes and 10 failures in *each* group

### Confidence Interval for $p_1 - p_2$

Since we don't know $p_1$ and $p_2$, we estimate the standard error by plugging in the sample proportions. The confidence interval for $p_1 - p_2$ is:

$$
(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) \pm z^* \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}
$$

When interpreting this interval, pay attention to whether it contains 0. If the entire interval is above 0, the data suggest $p_1$ is larger than $p_2$. If the entire interval is below 0, the data suggest $p_1$ is smaller. If the interval contains 0, then "no difference" is a plausible value.

**Example 4.5.1 – Confidence Interval for a Difference**

A survey asks randomly selected adults whether they regularly get news from social media. Of 600 adults aged 18–29, 438 said yes. Of 800 adults aged 50–64, 344 said yes.

1. Calculate $\hat{p}_1$ and $\hat{p}_2$.
2. Check the conditions.
3. Construct a 95% confidence interval for $p_1 - p_2$.
4. Interpret the interval in context. Does it suggest a real difference between the age groups?

<Solution>

1. $\hat{p}_1 = \dfrac{438}{600} = 0.73$ (ages 18–29) and $\hat{p}_2 = \dfrac{344}{800} = 0.43$ (ages 50–64)
2. **Independence:** random samples from two separate age groups. **Success-failure:** 438 and 162 in group 1, 344 and 456 in group 2, all at least 10.
3. Compute the standard error, then the interval:

$$
SE = \sqrt{\frac{0.73(0.27)}{600} + \frac{0.43(0.57)}{800}} \approx 0.0252
$$

$$
(0.73 - 0.43) \pm 1.96(0.0252) = 0.30 \pm 0.049 \quad\Rightarrow\quad (0.251, 0.349)
$$

4. We are 95% confident that the proportion of 18–29 year-olds who get news from social media is between **25.1 and 34.9 percentage points higher** than for 50–64 year-olds. The interval is entirely above 0, so there is a **real difference** between the age groups.

### Hypothesis Test for $p_1 - p_2$

The most common test for two proportions checks whether they are equal:

$$
H_0: p_1 = p_2 \quad \text{vs.} \quad H_a: p_1 \neq p_2 \; (\text{or} < \text{or} >)
$$

which we can also write as $H_0: p_1 - p_2 = 0$.

Just like in Section 4.4, we carry out the test assuming $H_0$ is true. If $H_0$ is true, the two groups share the same proportion, so our best estimate of that common proportion combines both samples. This is called the **pooled proportion**, written $\hat{p}_{pool}$ and pronounced "p-hat pool":

$$
\hat{p}_{pool} = \frac{x_1 + x_2}{n_1 + n_2}
$$

where $x_1$ and $x_2$ are the number of successes in each group. We use the pooled proportion both to check the success-failure condition and to compute the standard error. The test statistic is:

$$
z = \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\hat{p}_{pool}(1-\hat{p}_{pool})\left(\dfrac{1}{n_1} + \dfrac{1}{n_2}\right)}}
$$

We then find the p-value from the standard normal distribution and draw a conclusion, just as before. Statistical software can also carry out this test directly.

**Example 4.5.2 – An A/B Test**

An online store tests two versions of its product page. Visitors are randomly assigned to one of the versions:

| Version | Visitors | Purchases |
|---|---|---|
| A (original) | 1,500 | 96 |
| B (new layout) | 1,500 | 129 |

At $\alpha = 0.05$, is there evidence that the two versions have different purchase rates?

1. State $H_0$ and $H_a$.
2. Calculate the pooled proportion and check the conditions.
3. Calculate the test statistic and p-value.
4. State your conclusion in context. Since this was a randomized experiment, what can the store conclude about the new layout?

<Solution>

1. $H_0: p_A = p_B$ vs. $H_a: p_A \neq p_B$
2. $\hat{p}_A = \dfrac{96}{1500} = 0.064$ and $\hat{p}_B = \dfrac{129}{1500} = 0.086$

$$
\hat{p}_{pool} = \frac{96 + 129}{1500 + 1500} = \frac{225}{3000} = 0.075
$$

   **Success-failure (pooled):** $1500(0.075) = 112.5$ and $1500(0.925) = 1387.5$, both at least 10. Visitors were randomly assigned, so independence is reasonable.

3. Compute the test statistic:

$$
z = \frac{0.086 - 0.064}{\sqrt{0.075(0.925)\left(\dfrac{1}{1500} + \dfrac{1}{1500}\right)}} = \frac{0.022}{0.00962} \approx 2.29
$$

   p-value $= 2 \times P(Z > 2.29) \approx 0.022$

4. Since $0.022 \leq 0.05$, we **reject $H_0$**. Version B had a significantly higher purchase rate (8.6% vs. 6.4%). **Because visitors were randomly assigned**, the store can conclude that the new layout *caused* the increase in purchases.

</Solution>

For each task, decide whether you should use the pooled proportion $\hat{p}_{pool}$ or the separate sample proportions $\hat{p}_1$ and $\hat{p}_2$ in the standard error, and explain why:

1. Constructing a 90% confidence interval for $p_1 - p_2$.
2. Testing $H_0: p_1 = p_2$.

<Solution>

1. **Separate proportions** $\hat{p}_1$ and $\hat{p}_2$. A confidence interval doesn't assume the proportions are equal.
2. **Pooled proportion.** The test is carried out assuming $H_0: p_1 = p_2$ is true, so we combine both samples to estimate the shared proportion.
3. **Pooled proportion.** As with the standard error, the condition is checked under the assumption that $H_0$ is true.

**Example 4.5.4 – Observational Data**

A researcher compares the proportion of Facebook posts that receive at least 100 likes for posts that include a photo versus posts that do not. They find a statistically significant difference, with photo posts receiving 100+ likes more often.

1. Was this a randomized experiment or an observational study?
2. Can the researcher conclude that adding a photo *causes* more likes? What other variables might explain the difference?

<Solution>

1. **Observational study.** The researcher didn't randomly assign which posts got photos; the posters chose.
2. **No.** Without random assignment, other variables could explain the difference. For example:
   - Posts with photos might come from **accounts with more followers**.
   - Photo posts might be about **more exciting events** (vacations, weddings, pets).
   - People who add photos might **post at better times** or put more effort into their posts overall.

   To show that photos *cause* more likes, the researcher would need a randomized experiment, such as randomly choosing whether each post includes a photo.

</Solution>

### Key Points – Difference of Two Proportions
- Comparing two proportions estimates $p_1 - p_2$ using $\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2$
- A/B tests randomly assign subjects to two versions, so a significant difference supports a cause-and-effect conclusion
- Conditions: independence within and between groups, and at least 10 successes and 10 failures in each group
- Confidence interval: $(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) \pm z^* \sqrt{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)/n_1 + \hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)/n_2}$; check whether it contains 0
- For a test of $H_0: p_1 = p_2$, use the pooled proportion $\hat{p}_{pool} = (x_1 + x_2)/(n_1 + n_2)$ in the standard error
- Variances add when taking a difference, so the difference is more uncertain than either single proportion
