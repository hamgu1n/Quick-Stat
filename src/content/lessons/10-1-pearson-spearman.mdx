# Unit 5 – Correlation and Regression

## Section 5.1 – Pearson and Spearman Correlation

In Section 2.8 we used scatterplots to *visualize* the relationship between two numerical variables. Now we want to *quantify* that relationship, to give it a single number that captures both its direction and its strength. That number is called a **correlation coefficient**.

### Pearson's Correlation Coefficient

The most widely used measure of linear association is **Pearson's correlation coefficient**, denoted $r$. It measures the strength and direction of the *linear* relationship between two numerical variables $X$ and $Y$.

The formula is:

$$
r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{(n-1) \cdot s_x \cdot s_y}
$$

where $s_x$ and $s_y$ are the sample standard deviations of $X$ and $Y$ respectively. In practice, software computes this for us, but understanding the formula gives insight into what $r$ actually measures: it asks how often $x$ and $y$ are *both* above their respective means (positive product) or both below (also positive), versus one above and one below (negative product).

The key properties of $r$:
- $-1 \leq r \leq 1$ always
- $r > 0$: positive linear association; $r < 0$: negative linear association
- $r = 1$ or $r = -1$: perfect linear relationship (all points on a line)
- $r = 0$: no *linear* relationship (but there could still be a curved relationship)
- $r$ is unitless and unaffected by changes in scale or location

**Example 5.1.2 – Calculating Pearson's r**

The following data show the number of hours five students studied and their quiz scores:

| Hours ($x$) | Score ($y$) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 55 |
| 2 | 65 |
| 3 | 70 |
| 4 | 80 |
| 5 | 85 |

$\bar{x} = 3$, $\bar{y} = 71$, $s_x = 1.58$, $s_y = 11.94$.

Calculate $r$ and interpret the result.

<Solution>

Compute the products of deviations, $(x - \bar{x})(y - \bar{y})$:

| $x$ | $y$ | $x - \bar{x}$ | $y - \bar{y}$ | Product |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 55 | −2 | −16 | 32 |
| 2 | 65 | −1 | −6 | 6 |
| 3 | 70 | 0 | −1 | 0 |
| 4 | 80 | 1 | 9 | 9 |
| 5 | 85 | 2 | 14 | 28 |

The products add to 75, so

$$
r = \frac{\sum (x - \bar{x})(y - \bar{y})}{(n - 1)\, s_x s_y} = \frac{75}{4(1.58)(11.94)} \approx 0.993
$$

**Interpretation:** $r \approx 0.99$ is a **very strong positive linear relationship**. Students who studied more hours scored higher on the quiz, and the points fall almost exactly on a line.

### Spearman's Rank Correlation

Pearson's $r$ assumes a *linear* relationship and works best when both variables are roughly normally distributed without extreme outliers. When these conditions are not met, for example, when the data are ordinal, heavily skewed, or contain outliers that would distort a linear measure, we turn to **Spearman's rank correlation coefficient**, denoted $r_s$ (pronounced "r sub s").

Spearman's $r_s$ is computed by first converting each variable's values to ranks (1 = smallest, $n$ = largest), then computing Pearson's $r$ on those ranks. A simplified formula is:

$$
r_s = 1 - \frac{6 \sum d_i^2}{n(n^2 - 1)}
$$

where $d_i$ is the difference between the ranks of the $i$-th observation in $X$ and $Y$.

Like Pearson's $r$, Spearman's $r_s$ ranges from $-1$ to $+1$. The difference is that Spearman's $r_s$ measures *monotonic* association, whether $Y$ tends to increase as $X$ increases, regardless of whether the relationship is linear.

<div className="example">
**Example 5.1.3 – When to Use Spearman vs. Pearson**

For each scenario, decide whether Pearson's $r$ or Spearman's $r_s$ is more appropriate:

1. Researchers measure the height and weight of 50 randomly selected adults with no obvious outliers.
2. A professor ranks students by GPA and also records their ranking in a class participation survey.
3. A dataset of house prices contains several extreme values (mansions worth several million dollars).
4. A study records the number of hours slept and a continuous cognitive performance score for 100 participants, and a preliminary scatterplot suggests a roughly linear relationship.

<Solution>

1. **Pearson's $r$**: two numerical variables with a roughly linear relationship and no outliers.
2. **Spearman's $r_s$**: the data are already ranks (ordinal).
3. **Spearman's $r_s$**: extreme values would distort Pearson's $r$, but ranks are resistant to them.
4. **Pearson's $r$**: both variables are numerical, the relationship looks linear, and the sample is large.

</Solution>

<div className="example">
**Example 5.1.4 – Calculating Spearman's r_s**

Five runners finish a 5K race. Their times (in minutes) and their training hours per week are:

| Runner | Time | Training hrs/wk |
|---|---|---|
| A | 22 | 10 |
| B | 18 | 15 |
| C | 25 | 8 |
| D | 20 | 12 |
| E | 16 | 18 |

Rank each variable, compute the rank differences $d_i$, and calculate Spearman's $r_s$.

<Solution>

Rank each variable from smallest (1) to largest (5):

| Runner | Time | Rank (time) | Training | Rank (training) | $d_i$ | $d_i^2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 22 | 4 | 10 | 2 | 2 | 4 |
| B | 18 | 2 | 15 | 4 | −2 | 4 |
| C | 25 | 5 | 8 | 1 | 4 | 16 |
| D | 20 | 3 | 12 | 3 | 0 | 0 |
| E | 16 | 1 | 18 | 5 | −4 | 16 |

$\sum d_i^2 = 40$, so

$$
r_s = 1 - \frac{6 \sum d_i^2}{n(n^2 - 1)} = 1 - \frac{6(40)}{5(24)} = 1 - 2 = -1
$$

$r_s = -1$, a **perfect negative monotonic relationship**. The more a runner trained, the faster (lower) their time, with no exceptions in the ranking.

</Solution>
</div>

### Key Points – Pearson and Spearman Correlation
- Pearson's $r$ measures the strength and direction of a *linear* relationship; ranges from $-1$ to $+1$
- $r = 0$ indicates no linear relationship, but a strong curved relationship may still exist
- Pearson's $r$ is sensitive to outliers and assumes roughly linear, normally distributed data
- Spearman's $r_s$ measures *monotonic* association using ranks, more robust to outliers and non-normality
- Both coefficients share the $[-1, 1]$ range and the same directional interpretation
- Neither coefficient alone establishes causation, only appropriate study design can do that
