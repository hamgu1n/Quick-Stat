## Section 5.2 – Interpreting Correlation Coefficients

Computing a correlation coefficient is straightforward; interpreting it correctly is where students often go wrong. In this section we address the most common pitfalls, the difference between association and causation and the effect of outliers.

### Strength Guidelines

While there are no universal rules, the following guidelines are widely used to describe the strength of a correlation:

| $\lvert r \rvert$ value | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| 0.00 – 0.19 | Very weak or no association |
| 0.20 – 0.39 | Weak association |
| 0.40 – 0.59 | Moderate association |
| 0.60 – 0.79 | Strong association |
| 0.80 – 1.00 | Very strong association |

These are guidelines, not rules. The appropriate threshold for "strong" depends on the field, an $r$ of 0.5 might be impressive in psychology but unacceptably weak in physics.

<div className="example">
**Example 5.2.1 – Describing a Correlation**

For each correlation coefficient, describe the strength and direction of the relationship:

1. $r = 0.87$ between number of practice problems completed and final exam score.
2. $r = -0.43$ between daily screen time and academic GPA.
3. $r = 0.12$ between a student's height and their statistics grade.
4. $r = -0.91$ between the age of a car and its fuel efficiency.

<Solution>

1. $r = 0.87$: **strong positive**
2. $r = -0.43$: **moderate negative**
3. $r = 0.12$: **very weak positive** (essentially no association)
4. $r = -0.91$: **very strong negative**

</Solution>
</div>

### Correlation Does Not Imply Causation

This is perhaps the most important lesson in all of statistics: **a high correlation between two variables does not mean that one causes the other.** We saw a hint of this in Section 2.8 with the ice cream and drowning example. Here we formalize the idea.

Three possible explanations for an observed correlation between $X$ and $Y$ are:

1. $X$ causes $Y$
2. $Y$ causes $X$
3. A third variable $Z$ (a **confounding variable** or **lurking variable**) causes both $X$ and $Y$

The ice cream and drowning example is driven by a lurking variable: hot weather. Hot weather causes people to buy more ice cream *and* causes more people to swim, increasing drowning risk. Ice cream consumption doesn't cause drowning.

Only a properly designed experiment (with randomization and control, as discussed in Section 1.5) can establish causation. A correlation from an observational study, no matter how strong, cannot.

<div className="example">
**Example 5.2.2 – Causation or Lurking Variable?**

For each observed correlation, propose a possible lurking variable or alternative explanation:

1. Cities with more hospitals tend to have higher death rates.
2. Countries with more televisions per household tend to have longer life expectancies.
3. Students who eat breakfast score higher on standardized tests than those who skip breakfast.
4. The number of firefighters at a fire scene is positively correlated with the amount of damage done.

<Solution>

1. **Population size.** Bigger cities have more hospitals *and* more deaths. Also, sick people travel to cities with many hospitals for treatment.
2. **Wealth.** Wealthier countries can afford more televisions *and* better healthcare, nutrition, and sanitation.
3. **Family background or overall habits.** Students who eat breakfast may have more structured home lives, more family support, or better sleep, any of which could raise test scores.
4. **Size of the fire.** Bigger fires bring more firefighters *and* cause more damage. The firefighters aren't causing the damage.

</Solution>
</div>

### The Effect of Outliers on Correlation

Pearson's $r$ is sensitive to outliers, as we noted in the previous section. A single unusual point can dramatically increase or decrease $r$, even when the rest of the data show no clear linear pattern. This is one reason why always *looking at the scatterplot* before reporting $r$ is critical. The correlation alone can be deeply misleading.

<div className="example">
**Example 5.2.3 – Outlier Impact on r**

A researcher computes $r = 0.82$ for a dataset of 20 points. Upon examining the scatterplot, she notices that removing a single outlier reduces $r$ to 0.31.

What does this tell us about the reliability of the original correlation? What should be done before reporting the result?

<Solution>

The original correlation is **not reliable**. A single point was responsible for most of the apparent relationship, and without it, the remaining 19 points show only a weak association ($r = 0.31$).

Before reporting, the researcher should:

- **investigate the outlier**: is it a data-entry error, a measurement problem, or a genuine but unusual case?
- **report results both with and without it** (or explain clearly why it was removed)
- **always show the scatterplot** alongside $r$

</Solution>
</div>

### Testing Whether a Correlation Is Significant

A computed correlation coefficient is a sample statistic, it varies from sample to sample. Just because $r \neq 0$ in our sample doesn't guarantee that the true population correlation $\rho$ (the Greek letter "rho") is nonzero. We can perform a hypothesis test:

$$
H_0: \rho = 0 \quad \text{(no linear relationship in the population)}
$$
$$
H_a: \rho \neq 0 \quad \text{(or one-sided)}
$$

The test statistic is:

$$
t = \frac{r\sqrt{n-2}}{\sqrt{1-r^2}}
$$

which follows a t-distribution with $n - 2$ degrees of freedom under $H_0$. The p-value tells us whether the observed $r$ is large enough, for the given sample size, to be statistically unlikely if there truly is no relationship.

<div className="example">
**Example 5.2.4 – Testing Correlation Significance**

A sample of $n = 20$ observations yields a Pearson correlation of $r = 0.45$.

1. Calculate the t-test statistic.
2. With $df = 18$ and $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-tailed), the critical value is approximately 2.101. Is the correlation statistically significant?
3. What does "statistically significant" mean in this context?

<Solution>

1. Compute the test statistic:

$$
t = \frac{r\sqrt{n - 2}}{\sqrt{1 - r^2}} = \frac{0.45\sqrt{18}}{\sqrt{1 - 0.2025}} = \frac{1.909}{0.893} \approx 2.14
$$

2. **Yes.** Since $2.14 > 2.101$, we reject $H_0: \rho = 0$. (The exact p-value is about 0.046.)
3. It means a correlation as strong as 0.45 would be **unlikely to occur by chance** in a sample of 20 if there were truly no linear relationship in the population. It does *not* mean the relationship is strong, or that one variable causes the other.

</Solution>
</div>

<div className="example">
**Example 5.2.5 – Sample Size and Significance**

Explain why a correlation of $r = 0.30$ might be statistically significant with $n = 100$ but not with $n = 15$, even though the correlation coefficient is the same.

<Solution>

The test statistic $t = \dfrac{r\sqrt{n-2}}{\sqrt{1-r^2}}$ grows with $\sqrt{n - 2}$:

- **$n = 100$:** $t = \dfrac{0.30\sqrt{98}}{\sqrt{0.91}} \approx 3.11$, so $p \approx 0.002$, which is significant.
- **$n = 15$:** $t = \dfrac{0.30\sqrt{13}}{\sqrt{0.91}} \approx 1.13$, so $p \approx 0.28$, which is not significant.

With a small sample, a correlation of 0.30 could easily happen by chance even when there is no real relationship. With 100 observations, the same correlation is much harder to explain away as luck. Larger samples give more evidence, so smaller effects can be detected.

</Solution>
</div>

### Key Points – Interpreting Correlation Coefficients
- Strength guidelines: $|r| < 0.20$ weak, $0.40$ – $0.59$ moderate, $\geq 0.80$ very strong (field-dependent)
- Correlation describes association, it does not establish causation
- A lurking variable may drive both variables simultaneously, producing correlation without direct causation
- Always inspect the scatterplot, outliers can dramatically distort $r$
<Advanced compact>

- Statistical significance of $r$ depends on both the size of $r$ and the sample size $n$
- A significant correlation with a large sample may still represent a practically unimportant relationship, always consider effect size alongside p-values

</Advanced>
