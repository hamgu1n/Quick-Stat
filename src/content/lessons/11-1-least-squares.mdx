## Section 5.3 – The Least Squares Method

Correlation tells us the *strength* and *direction* of a linear relationship, but it doesn't give us a way to make predictions. If we know that hours studied and exam scores are strongly correlated, we still can't say "if a student studies 6 hours, what score should we expect?" For that, we need a line, specifically, the **least squares regression line**.

### The Simple Linear Regression Model

**Simple linear regression** models the relationship between one explanatory variable $X$ and one response variable $Y$ using a straight line:

$$
\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x
$$

where $\hat{y}$ (read "y-hat") is the **predicted value** of $Y$ for a given $X$, $b_0$ (pronounced "b-naught") is the **y-intercept**, and $b_1$ ("b-one") is the **slope**, the predicted change in $Y$ for a one-unit increase in $X$.

The hat on $\hat{y}$ is important: it reminds us that we are predicting an *average* response, not an exact one. Individual observations will vary around the line.

### Finding the Least Squares Line

Among all possible straight lines we could draw through a scatterplot, the **least squares line** is the one that minimizes the sum of the squared **residuals**. A **residual** for observation $i$, written $e_i$ (pronounced "e sub i"), is the difference between the observed and predicted values:

$$
e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i
$$

The least squares line minimizes $\sum e_i^2$, the sum of squared residuals. The formulas for the slope and intercept are:

$$
b_1 = r \cdot \frac{s_y}{s_x}
$$

$$
b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1 \bar{x}
$$

where $r$ is Pearson's correlation, $s_y$ and $s_x$ are the sample standard deviations of $Y$ and $X$, and $\bar{y}$, $\bar{x}$ are the sample means. Note that the regression line always passes through the point $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$, the means of the two variables.

**Example 5.3.1 – Calculating the Regression Line**

For a dataset of 10 students, the following summary statistics are recorded:

- $\bar{x} = 4$ hours studied, $\bar{y} = 72$ points
- $s_x = 1.5$, $s_y = 8$
- $r = 0.85$

Find the equation of the least squares regression line.

<Solution>

$$
b_1 = r\frac{s_y}{s_x} = 0.85 \cdot \frac{8}{1.5} \approx 4.533
$$

$$
b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1\bar{x} = 72 - 4.533(4) \approx 53.87
$$

$$
\hat{y} = 53.87 + 4.53x
$$

**Example 5.3.2 – Interpreting Slope and Intercept**

The regression equation for predicting a student's final exam score ($\hat{y}$) from hours studied ($x$) is:

$$
\hat{y} = 48.5 + 4.53x
$$

1. Interpret the slope in the context of the problem.
2. Interpret the y-intercept. Does it have a meaningful practical interpretation here?
3. Predict the exam score for a student who studies 7 hours.

<Solution>

1. **Slope:** for each additional hour studied, the predicted exam score **increases by about 4.53 points**.
2. **Intercept:** a student who studies 0 hours is predicted to score **48.5**. This is meaningful only if the data include students who studied close to 0 hours. If they don't, it is an extrapolation and shouldn't be taken literally.
3. $\hat{y} = 48.5 + 4.53(7) = 48.5 + 31.71 = 80.21$, so about **80 points**.

**Example 5.3.3 – Residuals**

Using the regression line from Example 5.3.2, a student studied 5 hours and scored 76 on the exam.

1. What is the predicted score for a student who studied 5 hours?
2. Calculate the residual for this student.
3. Is the residual positive or negative, and what does that mean?

3. The residual is **positive**, so the student scored **4.85 points higher than the model predicted**. The point lies above the regression line.

### Extrapolation

The regression line is only reliable for predictions within the range of the observed data, or close to it. **Extrapolation** means making predictions far outside the range of $X$ values used to fit the model. This is dangerous because we have no evidence the linear trend continues beyond our data. For example, if our study hours data ranged from 1 to 10 hours, predicting the score for 50 hours of studying is extrapolation, the relationship might level off, reverse, or behave completely differently.

<div className="example">
**Example 5.3.4 – Dangers of Extrapolation**

A regression model predicts monthly revenue ($\hat{y}$, in thousands of dollars) from the number of employees ($x$) for small businesses with 5 to 30 employees:

$$
\hat{y} = 12 + 3.8x
$$

A startup plans to hire 150 employees and uses this equation to project revenues. What is the concern with this prediction?

<Solution>

This is **extrapolation**. The model was built using businesses with 5 to 30 employees, and 150 is far outside that range. We have no evidence that the relationship stays linear at that size. Larger companies may have different costs, markets, and efficiencies. The prediction, $12 + 3.8(150) = 582$ thousand dollars per month, could be badly wrong.

</Solution>
</div>

<div className="example">
**Example 5.3.5 – Full Regression Problem**

A dataset of 8 cities records average January temperature ($x$, in °F) and heating oil consumption ($y$, in gallons per household per month):

| City | Temp ($x$) | Oil ($y$) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 20 | 45 |
| B | 25 | 40 |
| C | 30 | 38 |
| D | 35 | 32 |
| E | 40 | 28 |
| F | 45 | 24 |
| G | 50 | 20 |
| H | 55 | 17 |

$\bar{x} = 37.5$, $\bar{y} = 30.5$, $s_x = 12.25$, $s_y = 10.0$, $r = -0.997$.

Find the regression equation and predict heating oil consumption for a city with an average January temperature of 28°F.

<Solution>

$$
b_1 = r\frac{s_y}{s_x} = -0.997 \cdot \frac{10.0}{12.25} \approx -0.814
$$

$$
b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1\bar{x} = 30.5 - (-0.814)(37.5) \approx 61.02
$$

$$
\hat{y} = 61.02 - 0.814x
$$

**Prediction at 28°F:** $\hat{y} = 61.02 - 0.814(28) \approx 38.2$ gallons per household per month.

The slope means each 1°F increase in average January temperature is associated with about 0.81 fewer gallons of heating oil per month.

</Solution>
</div>

### Key Points – Least Squares Method
- The least squares line minimizes the sum of squared residuals: $\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$
- Slope: $b_1 = r \cdot s_y / s_x$, the predicted change in $Y$ per one-unit increase in $X$
- Intercept: $b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1\bar{x}$, the predicted $Y$ when $X = 0$
- The line always passes through $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$
- Residuals measure prediction error: $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$; positive residuals lie above the line, negative below
- Avoid extrapolation, predictions far outside the range of observed $X$ values are unreliable
