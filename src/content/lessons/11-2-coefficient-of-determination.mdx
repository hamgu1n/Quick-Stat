## Section 11.2 – The Coefficient of Determination

The least squares regression line gives us the best linear prediction of $Y$ from $X$. But how good is that prediction, really? The **coefficient of determination**, denoted $R^2$ (or $r^2$ in simple linear regression), measures exactly this, the proportion of the total variability in $Y$ that is explained by the linear relationship with $X$.

### What Is $R^2$?

The idea behind $R^2$ is to compare two scenarios:

1. **No model**: our best prediction of $Y$ is simply its mean $\bar{y}$. The total variability in $Y$ around this baseline is called the **total sum of squares (SST)**:

$$
\text{SST} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \bar{y})^2
$$

2. **With the regression model**: our prediction is $\hat{y}_i$. The remaining variability, the variability *not* explained by the model, is the **residual sum of squares (SSE)**:

$$
\text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2
$$

The coefficient of determination is:

$$
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} = \frac{\text{SST} - \text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}
$$

In simple linear regression, $R^2 = r^2$, the square of the Pearson correlation coefficient. So if $r = 0.9$, then $R^2 = 0.81$, meaning 81% of the variability in $Y$ is explained by its linear relationship with $X$.

$R^2$ ranges from 0 to 1. An $R^2$ of 0 means the model explains none of the variability (the regression line is no better than just using $\bar{y}$). An $R^2$ of 1 means the model explains all the variability (every point falls exactly on the line).

<div className="example">
**Example 11.2.1 – Calculating $R^2$**

A regression model predicting exam scores from study hours has SST = 1,200 and SSE = 240.

1. Calculate $R^2$.
2. Interpret this value in context.
3. What is the Pearson correlation $r$? (Assume a positive relationship.)

<Solution>

1. $R^2 = 1 - \dfrac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \dfrac{240}{1200} = 0.80$
2. **80% of the variability in exam scores is explained** by the linear relationship with study hours. The other 20% comes from other factors.
3. $r = +\sqrt{0.80} \approx 0.894$

</Solution>
</div>

**Example 11.2.2 – Interpreting $R^2$**

A regression of monthly advertising spend ($x$) on sales revenue ($y$) for a small chain of coffee shops yields $r = 0.73$.

1. Calculate $R^2$.
2. Interpret $R^2$ in a sentence.
3. What accounts for the remaining variability in sales that is *not* explained by advertising spend?

<Solution>

1. $R^2 = 0.73^2 \approx 0.533$
2. About **53.3% of the variation in sales revenue** across shops is explained by the linear relationship with advertising spend.
3. The remaining 46.7% comes from **other factors**, such as location, foot traffic, prices, competition, season, menu, and staff, plus random month-to-month variation.

</Solution>

### $R^2$ Does Not Tell the Whole Story

A high $R^2$ sounds reassuring, but it does not guarantee the regression model is appropriate. Several cautions apply:

**A high $R^2$ does not prove causation.** As we emphasized in Chapter 10, a strong linear relationship may be driven by lurking variables.

**A high $R^2$ does not mean the model fits well.** If the true relationship is curved, fitting a straight line may still produce a high $R^2$ while systematically mispredicting across the range of $X$. This is why we examine residual plots (Section 11.3).

**A low $R^2$ does not make a model useless.** In complex fields like psychology or economics, $R^2$ values of 0.10 to 0.30 are common and the model may still be meaningful, especially if the slope is statistically significant and the relationship is theoretically justified.

**Example 11.2.3 – High $R^2$, Wrong Model**

A dataset relating age ($x$, in years) to annual salary ($y$, in thousands of dollars) produces a regression line with $R^2 = 0.78$. However, a scatterplot reveals that salary rises steeply from ages 25 to 45, then levels off after 50.

What does this suggest about using a linear model here? Would a high $R^2$ guarantee good predictions across all ages?

<Solution>

A linear model is the **wrong shape** for these data. The relationship is curved: it rises steeply and then levels off. A straight line will systematically **underpredict** salaries in the middle years and **overpredict** them for older workers, where the real salaries have flattened out.

**No**, a high $R^2$ doesn't guarantee good predictions across all ages. $R^2$ only measures how much variation the line explains *overall*. A residual plot here would show a curved pattern, which is the sign of a poor model fit.

</Solution>
</div>

<div className="example">
**Example 11.2.4 – Comparing Models**

Two researchers each build a regression model to predict students' GPA from the number of hours they study per week.

- Researcher A's model: $R^2 = 0.65$
- Researcher B's model: $R^2 = 0.40$

Researcher B argues her model is still useful. Under what circumstances might she be right? What additional information would help you evaluate both models?

<Solution>

Researcher B could be right in several situations:

- The models might use **different data**, for example different schools or populations. B's population may simply be more varied, so explaining 40% of it is still useful.
- **In many fields, $R^2 = 0.40$ is quite good**, since human behavior is affected by many factors.
- B's model might **meet the regression assumptions** better. A higher $R^2$ means nothing if the model is the wrong shape or was fit to flawed data.

Information that would help:

- the residual plots for both models
- the sample sizes and how each sample was collected
- whether the relationships are statistically significant
- prediction errors on new data the models haven't seen
- whether either model was fit to data with influential outliers

</Solution>

### Key Points – Coefficient of Determination
- $R^2$ measures the proportion of variability in $Y$ explained by the linear relationship with $X$
- $R^2 = 1 - \text{SSE}/\text{SST}$; ranges from 0 to 1; in simple regression, $R^2 = r^2$
- $R^2 = 1$: perfect fit; $R^2 = 0$: model no better than using $\bar{y}$
- A high $R^2$ does not imply causation or guarantee the model is appropriate
- A low $R^2$ does not automatically mean the model is useless, significance and context matter
- Always examine residual plots alongside $R^2$ to assess whether a linear model is appropriate
