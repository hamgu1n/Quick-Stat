### How is $P(A \mid B)$ read?
@ definition
- The probability of $A$ and $B$
- The probability of $A$ or $B$
- The probability of $A$ divided by $B$
+ The probability of $A$ given that $B$ has occurred
> The vertical bar means "given." We restrict attention to outcomes where $B$ happened.

### What is the formula for $P(A \mid B)$?
@ definition
+ $\frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$
- $P(A) \times P(B)$
- $\frac{P(A)}{P(B)}$
- $P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$
> We divide the overlap by the probability of the condition, $B$.

### $P(A \cap B) = 0.12$ and $P(B) = 0.4$. What is $P(A \mid B)$?
@ definition
- 0.048
- 0.52
+ 0.3
- 0.28
> $P(A \mid B) = 0.12 / 0.4 = 0.3$.

### Of 200 students, 110 have a job, and 80 of those also own a laptop. Given a student has a job, what is the probability they own a laptop?
@ definition
- $\frac{80}{200}$
- $\frac{110}{200}$
+ $\frac{80}{110}$
- $\frac{80}{140}$
> Restrict to the 110 students with a job; 80 of them own a laptop: $80/110 \approx 0.727$.

### Using conditional probability, when are $A$ and $B$ independent?
@ conditional-probability-and-independence
+ When $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$
- When $P(A \mid B) = 0$
- When $P(A \mid B) = 1$
- When $P(A \mid B) = P(B)$
> Knowing $B$ happened gives no new information about $A$.

### Of 100 people, 40 sleep well overall, but 30 of the 50 people who exercise sleep well. Are exercising and sleeping well independent?
@ conditional-probability-and-independence
- Yes, because 30 is less than 40
+ No, because $P(\text{sleep well} \mid \text{exercise}) = 0.6$ but $P(\text{sleep well}) = 0.4$
- Yes, because half the people exercise
- We can't tell without more data
> The conditional probability differs from the overall probability, so the events are dependent.

### What does Bayes' Theorem let us do?
@ bayes-theorem
- Add the probabilities of two events
- Find the probability of a complement
+ Reverse the direction of conditioning: find $P(B \mid A)$ from $P(A \mid B)$
- Count the outcomes in a sample space
> Bayes' Theorem: $P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B)}{P(A)}$.

### A disease affects 10% of people. A test is positive for 80% of people with the disease and 10% of people without it. If someone tests positive, about what is the probability they have the disease?
@ bayes-theorem
- 0.80
+ 0.47
- 0.10
- 0.17
> $P(T^+) = (0.8)(0.1) + (0.1)(0.9) = 0.08 + 0.09 = 0.17$. Then $P(D \mid T^+) = 0.08 / 0.17 \approx 0.47$.

### Why is the chance of having a rare disease after a positive test often surprisingly low?
@ bayes-theorem
- Medical tests are usually wrong
- Bayes' Theorem only works for common diseases
- The test's true positive rate is always low
+ False positives from the large healthy group can outnumber true positives from the small sick group
> When a disease is rare, even a small false positive rate produces many false positives.

### A bag has 4 red and 2 blue marbles. Two are drawn without replacement. What is the probability both are red?
@ definition
- $\frac{4}{9}$
- $\frac{2}{3}$
- $\frac{1}{3}$
+ $\frac{2}{5}$
> $P(\text{1st red}) = 4/6$, then $P(\text{2nd red} \mid \text{1st red}) = 3/5$. Multiply: $\frac{4}{6} \times \frac{3}{5} = \frac{12}{30} = \frac{2}{5}$.
