### What does the Central Limit Theorem say?
@ the-central-limit-theorem
+ For large enough $n$, the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ is approximately normal, whatever the population's shape
- Every population is normally distributed
- Larger samples always have a larger mean
- The sample mean always equals the population mean
> The CLT is about the distribution of sample means, not the population or a single sample.

### What is a common rule of thumb for a "large enough" sample for the CLT?
@ the-central-limit-theorem
+ $n \geq 30$
- $n \geq 5$
- $n \geq 10$
- $n \geq 1{,}000$
> $n \geq 30$ is a common guideline, though very skewed populations may need larger samples.

### Wait times are strongly right-skewed. For samples of $n = 64$, what is the shape of the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$?
@ the-central-limit-theorem
- Strongly right-skewed
- Strongly left-skewed
+ Approximately normal
- Uniform
> The population is skewed, but $n = 64$ is large enough for the CLT.

### A sample of 25 is drawn from a population that is itself normal. Is $\bar{x}$ normally distributed?
@ the-central-limit-theorem
- Only if $n \geq 30$
- No, never
- Only if the population is skewed
+ Yes, exactly normal for any sample size
> When the population is normal, $\bar{x}$ is normal no matter the sample size. The CLT isn't even needed.

### A sample of 5 is drawn from a heavily skewed population. Can we use the CLT to treat $\bar{x}$ as normal?
@ the-central-limit-theorem
+ No, $n = 5$ is far too small to overcome heavy skew
- Yes, the CLT works for any sample size
- Yes, as long as the population mean is known
- No, the CLT never applies to skewed populations
> The CLT applies to skewed populations too, but they need larger samples.

### What is the z-score formula for a sample mean?
@ using-the-normal-approximation
- $z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$
- $z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma \sqrt{n}}$
- $z = \frac{\mu - \bar{x}}{n}$
+ $z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$
> For a sample mean, divide by the standard error, $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, not by $\sigma$.

### A population has $\mu = 50$ and $\sigma = 12$. For samples of $n = 36$, what is the standard error?
@ using-the-normal-approximation
- 12
- 0.33
- 6
+ 2
> $12 / \sqrt{36} = 12 / 6 = 2$.

### With $\mu = 50$, $\sigma = 12$, and $n = 36$, about what is $P(\bar{x} > 54)$?
@ using-the-normal-approximation
- 0.37
- 0.16
+ 0.023
- 0.50
> $z = (54 - 50)/2 = 2$, and $P(Z > 2) = 1 - 0.977 \approx 0.023$.

### Why is the CLT so important?
@ the-central-limit-theorem
- It guarantees our sample is unbiased
+ It lets us use normal probability calculations for sample means from almost any population
- It shows that individual values are always normal
- It means we never need large samples
> The CLT is the foundation for the confidence intervals and hypothesis tests in the next units.

### Individual heights have $\sigma = 2.8$ inches. For a sample of 49 people, how does the spread of $\bar{x}$ compare to the spread of individual heights?
@ using-the-normal-approximation
- It is the same, 2.8 inches
+ It is smaller, $2.8 / 7 = 0.4$ inches
- It is larger, $2.8 \times 7 = 19.6$ inches
- It is 0
> Averages vary much less than individuals: the standard error is $\sigma / \sqrt{n} = 2.8 / 7 = 0.4$.
