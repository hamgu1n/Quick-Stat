### A company claims 80% of customers are satisfied. A consumer group suspects it's lower. What are the hypotheses?
@ setting-up-the-test
- $H_0: \hat{p} = 0.80$ vs. $H_a: \hat{p} < 0.80$
+ $H_0: p = 0.80$ vs. $H_a: p < 0.80$
- $H_0: p < 0.80$ vs. $H_a: p = 0.80$
- $H_0: p = 0.80$ vs. $H_a: p \neq 0.80$
> Hypotheses are about the population proportion $p$, never $\hat{p}$, and the null contains the equals sign.

### When checking the success-failure condition for a test, which proportion do you use?
@ conditions-for-the-test
- $\hat{p}$
+ $p_0$, the value claimed by $H_0$
- 0.5
- The pooled proportion
> The test assumes $H_0$ is true, so we check $np_0 \geq 10$ and $n(1 - p_0) \geq 10$.

### Testing $H_0: p = 0.02$ with $n = 300$: is the success-failure condition met?
@ conditions-for-the-test
- Yes, because $n \geq 30$
+ No, because $np_0 = 6$, which is less than 10
- Yes, because $n(1 - p_0) = 294$
- We can't tell without $\hat{p}$
> Both $np_0$ and $n(1-p_0)$ must be at least 10. Here $300(0.02) = 6$.

### Testing $H_0: p = 0.5$ with $n = 100$, a sample gives $\hat{p} = 0.6$. What is the z-statistic?
@ the-one-proportion-z-test
+ 2
- 1
- 0.1
- 4
> $SE = \sqrt{0.5(0.5)/100} = 0.05$, so $z = (0.6 - 0.5)/0.05 = 2$.

### In the one-proportion z-test, what goes in the standard error?
@ the-one-proportion-z-test
- $\sqrt{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})/n}$
- $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$
+ $\sqrt{p_0(1 - p_0)/n}$
- $p_0/n$
> The standard error is calculated assuming $H_0$ is true, so it uses $p_0$.

### For a two-tailed test with $z = 1.8$, how do you find the p-value?
@ the-one-proportion-z-test
- $P(Z > 1.8)$
- $P(Z < 1.8)$
- $1 - 2 \times P(Z > 1.8)$
+ $2 \times P(Z > 1.8)$
> A two-tailed test counts the area in both tails: $2 \times P(Z > |z|)$.

### A 95% confidence interval for $p$ is (0.512, 0.568). Would a two-tailed test at $\alpha = 0.05$ reject $H_0: p = 0.50$?

- Yes, because 0.50 is inside the interval
- We can't tell without the p-value

### Why use $p = 0.5$ when there's no prior estimate?
@ choosing-a-sample-size
+ $p(1-p)$ is largest at 0.5, giving the most conservative (largest) sample size
- It's always the true proportion
- It gives the smallest sample size
- It makes the success-failure condition fail
> Using 0.5 guarantees the margin of error won't exceed the target.

### A study of 2 million users finds a sign-up rate went from 8.0% to 8.1%, with p = 0.0001. Which is true?
@ statistical-vs-practical-significance
+ It is statistically significant but may not be practically significant
- The result isn't statistically significant
- A small p-value means the effect is large
- The sample is too small to tell
> Huge samples can detect tiny differences. Whether 0.1 percentage points matters depends on real-world costs and benefits.
