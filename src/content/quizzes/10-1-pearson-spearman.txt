Section Review: Pearson and Spearman Correlation. Format notes are in src/lib/quiz.ts.

### What does Pearson's $r$ measure?
@ pearson-s-correlation-coefficient
+ The strength and direction of the linear relationship between two numerical variables
- Whether one variable causes another
- The slope of the regression line
- The difference between two means
> $r$ summarizes a scatterplot's linear trend in one number.

### Which value is impossible for Pearson's $r$?
@ pearson-s-correlation-coefficient
+ $1.4$
- $-0.98$
- $0$
- $1$
> $r$ is always between $-1$ and $1$.

### What does $r = -1$ mean?
@ pearson-s-correlation-coefficient
- There is no relationship
- The relationship is weak and negative
+ The points fall exactly on a downward-sloping line
- One variable causes the other to decrease
> $r = \pm 1$ means a perfect linear relationship.

### A scatterplot shows a clear U-shaped pattern, and $r \approx 0$. What can you conclude?
@ pearson-s-correlation-coefficient
- There is no relationship at all
- The calculation is wrong
- The relationship is strongly positive
+ There is no linear relationship, but there may be a strong curved one
> $r$ only measures linear association, so it can miss curved patterns.

### Heights are converted from inches to centimeters. What happens to $r$ between height and weight?
@ pearson-s-correlation-coefficient
- It gets 2.54 times larger
+ It stays the same
- It gets smaller
- It becomes 0
> $r$ is unitless and unaffected by changes in scale.

### How is Spearman's $r_s$ computed?
@ spearman-s-rank-correlation
- Square Pearson's $r$
- Divide the covariance by the sample size
- Count the points above the line
+ Rank each variable, then measure the correlation of the ranks
> Working with ranks makes $r_s$ resistant to outliers.

### What kind of association does Spearman's $r_s$ measure?
@ spearman-s-rank-correlation
- Only linear association
+ Monotonic association: whether $Y$ tends to rise (or fall) as $X$ rises
- Only causal association
- Association between categorical variables
> A relationship can be monotonic without being a straight line.

### Which situation calls for Spearman's $r_s$ instead of Pearson's $r$?
@ spearman-s-rank-correlation
- Height and weight of 50 adults with no outliers
- Two numerical variables with a clear linear trend
+ House prices that include a few extreme mansions
- A large sample with no outliers
> Extreme values distort Pearson's $r$, but ranks resist them.

### Four observations have rank differences whose squares add to $\sum d_i^2 = 2$. What is $r_s$?
@ spearman-s-rank-correlation
+ 0.8
- 0.2
- 0.5
- −0.8
> $r_s = 1 - \frac{6(2)}{4(16 - 1)} = 1 - \frac{12}{60} = 0.8$.

### Every runner who trained more finished faster, with no exceptions. What is Spearman's $r_s$ between training hours and finish time?
@ spearman-s-rank-correlation
- 1
- 0
- It can't be computed
+ −1
> More training always goes with a lower time: a perfect negative monotonic relationship.
