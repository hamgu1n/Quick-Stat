### Using the textbook's guidelines, how would you describe $r = 0.87$?
@ strength-guidelines
+ Very strong positive
- Moderate positive
- Strong negative
- Weak positive
> $|r|$ from 0.80 to 1.00 is very strong, and the positive sign gives the direction.

### How would you describe $r = -0.43$?
@ strength-guidelines
- Strong negative
- Weak positive
+ Moderate negative
- Very weak negative
> $|r|$ from 0.40 to 0.59 is moderate.

### How would you describe $r = 0.12$?
@ strength-guidelines
- Moderate positive
+ Very weak or no association
- Strong positive
- Perfect positive
> $|r|$ below 0.20 is very weak.

### Why are the strength guidelines "guidelines, not rules"?
@ strength-guidelines
- Correlations can be larger than 1
+ What counts as strong depends on the field
- The guidelines only apply to Spearman's $r_s$
- Strength doesn't matter
> An $r$ of 0.5 might be impressive in psychology but weak in physics.

### Cities with more hospitals have higher death rates. What's the most likely explanation?
@ correlation-does-not-imply-causation
- Hospitals cause deaths
- Deaths cause hospitals to be built
+ A lurking variable, population size, increases both
- The correlation must be a mistake
> Bigger cities have more hospitals and more deaths.

### Which is NOT one of the possible explanations for a correlation between $X$ and $Y$?
@ correlation-does-not-imply-causation
- $X$ causes $Y$
- $Y$ causes $X$
- A lurking variable causes both
+ A strong correlation always proves $X$ causes $Y$
> Correlation alone never proves causation.

### What kind of study can establish cause and effect?
@ correlation-does-not-imply-causation
- An observational study with a very strong correlation
- Any study with a large sample
- A survey with a high response rate
+ A properly designed experiment with randomization and control
> No matter how strong, a correlation from observational data can't show causation.

### Removing one outlier changes $r$ from 0.82 to 0.31. What does this tell you?
@ the-effect-of-outliers-on-correlation
+ One point created most of the apparent relationship
- The original correlation was reliable
- The outlier should always be deleted
- The remaining data are strongly correlated
> Investigate the outlier and report results with and without it.

### What should you always do before reporting a correlation?
@ the-effect-of-outliers-on-correlation
- Square it
- Round it to one decimal place
- Make sure it's positive
+ Look at the scatterplot
> A scatterplot reveals outliers and curved patterns that $r$ alone can hide.

### The number of firefighters at a fire is positively correlated with the damage done. What's the best explanation?
@ correlation-does-not-imply-causation
+ The size of the fire drives both
- Firefighters cause damage
- Damage summons more firefighters, so damage causes firefighters
- There is no real relationship
> Bigger fires bring more firefighters and cause more damage.
